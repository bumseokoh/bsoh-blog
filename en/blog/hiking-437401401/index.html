Baekdudaegan Ridge Linking Seokgibong and Samdobong Peaks: Minjujisan, North Chungcheong

개요

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization Photo Gallery (Gojari, Wonchon-myeon, Yeongdong-gun, North Chungcheong Province) · KOGL Type 1

Minjujisan (widely cited at around 1,242m) rises where North Chungcheong’s Yeongdong County meets North Jeolla’s Muju County and North Gyeongsang’s Gimcheon, centered on the Mulhan-ri area of Wonchon-myeon in Yeongdong. It’s part of a broader mountain cluster, its ridgeline connecting to nearby Seokgibong, Samdobong, and Gakhosan, and it’s also well known as a section of the Baekdudaegan main ridge. According to trail GIS data, several survey points near the summit range from 1,207 to 1,275m, with the highest recorded elevation on course data coming in at 1,275m. Overall it’s a moderate hike, but most of the routes to the summit run a long 9–12km one way, so it takes a real physical toll. A shorter course can be done as a day hike, but if you’re planning the traverse linking Seokgibong, Samdobong, and Gakhosan, it’s worth building in an overnight stay.

Mountain Shape and Terrain

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization Tourism Photo Gallery (1212 Mulhangyegok-ro, Sangchon-myeon, Yeongdong-gun, Chungcheongbuk-do) · Korea Open Government License

Minjujisan is mostly a soft-shouldered “soil mountain,” but around the summit area the terrain shifts, mixing in rocky ridgelines of various sizes. Looking at the peak points logged in the trail’s spatial data, we find Seokgibong (recorded at two points, 1,255m and 1,274m), Samdobong (1,207m), Gakhosan (1,227m), Minjujisan itself (logged at several points — 1,271m, 1,233m, and 1,243m), and the summit marker (1,217m). That tells you the summit isn’t a single sharp peak but more of a chain — several peaks and marker points strung along the ridge. Samdobong is especially well known as the spot where the borders of Chungcheongbuk-do, Jeollabuk-do, and Gyeongsangbuk-do all meet, and the Gakhosan side leans more rocky, with a bigger share of exposed ridge and, reportedly, plenty of stretches with open views. Down in the valley, meanwhile, Mulhangyegok is a classic soil-mountain valley — dense forest paired with clear mountain streams.

Feel of the Trail

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization photo gallery (Sangchon-myeon, Yeongdong-gun, Chungcheongbuk-do) · KOGL Type 1

The early stretch of the hike, whether you start from Mulhan Valley or head in from the Domaryeong side, mostly climbs gently through forest trails. Along the valley sections, the shade of broadleaf trees mixes with the sound of running water, keeping things noticeably cool even in the height of summer. As you near the ridge, rocky terrain starts showing up around Gakhosan and Seokgibong, opening up the views — and once you’re on the Baekdudaegan ridgeline itself, you get that layered, mountains-upon-mountains panorama stretching out in every direction. In fall, the foliage is said to spread wide across the ridge, and in winter the summit ridge often gets coated in snow and rime frost, giving it a real winter-mountain feel. Overall the trail runs pretty long, so this isn’t really a hike for a quick dash to the summit and back — it’s better suited to clearing your whole day and just settling into the rhythm of the ridge.

코스 1 — 갈림길 → 정상

📏 9.96km ⛰️ 724m gain ⏱️ 3:42 ➡️ One-way Difficulty ★★★★★ ⤓ GPX

Trailhead access: The trailhead for this course, “Gallim-gil” (the fork), is a junction point where this route meets other hiking trails, and it looks like most hikers reach it by coming in from the Mulhan Valley side. Public transit isn’t especially convenient in this area, so driving in from Yeongdong or Muju is generally the recommended approach. It’s a good idea to double-check the exact access road on a map app beforehand.

Features & who it’s for: Of the three courses, this is the shortest and most direct route to the summit — the path is simple enough that there aren’t even any notable waypoints to record along the way. The climb is steady pretty much from the start, so pacing yourself matters here. This one suits hikers who just want an efficient there-and-back trip to the summit, rather than a full ridge traverse.

Course 2 — Domaryeong Trailhead → Gakhosan Peak

📏 8.78km ⛰️ 734m gain ⏱️ 3:25 ➡️ One-way Difficulty ★★★★★ ⤓ GPX

Route: Domaryeong Trailhead → Domaryeong Entrance Parking Lot → Trail Start → Domaryeong Entrance → Trailhead → Sangyongjeong Pavilion → Gakhosan → Gakhosan Peak → Fork → Shelter → Rest Area → Minjujisan → Restroom → Bottom of the Premium Forest Trail → Hwangnyongsa Temple

Getting to the trailhead: Domaryeong is a well-known pass where a local road crests, and since the Domaryeong Entrance Parking Lot is right along the route, it’s fairly easy to reach by car. If you’re relying on public transit, you’ll likely need to get from the intercity bus terminal in Yeongdong or Muju to the nearest town center first, then find further transport toward Domaryeong — it’s worth checking the routes ahead of time.

Highlights & who it’s for: This is a long ridge-traverse course — you climb Gakhosan Peak first, then work your way along the ridge past a shelter, a rest area, Minjujisan, and a restroom, among other points. You get the rocky-ridge views that Gakhosan is known for, plus the scenery from Minjujisan’s summit, all in one hike. It’s a good pick if you enjoy ridge walking and like your hike to serve up a variety of scenery.

Course 3 — Fork → Seokgibong

Route: Fork → Rest area → Minjujisan → Samsinsang rest area → Seokgibong → Helipad/rest area → Samdobong → Eumjuam Falls → Restroom → Third restroom → Second restroom

Trailhead access: This one looks like it starts from the Mulhan Valley side, and just like the previous two courses, getting there is generally easier by car than by public transit.

Highlights & who it’s for: This is the longest of the three courses, linking Minjujisan, Seokgibong, and Samdobong while tracing the ridgeline of the Baekdudaegan. Along the way you also pass valley scenery like Eumjuam Falls, and with restrooms marked at several points, it’s a course that makes long-distance hiking a bit more convenient. All told, it suits experienced hikers with the stamina and time to spare.

Difficulty, Terrain & Cautions

The table above reflects total trail length within the district (41.09km) as aggregated by administrative division — it’s not the distance of a single course, but a sum of several separate sections. According to the data, the highest recorded difficulty is “Moderate,” and the surface material isn’t broken out separately. That said, the summit-area course covered earlier tells a different story: based on actual GPX measurements, it involves over 700m of net elevation gain and a one-way distance of 9-12km, so the real, on-the-ground difficulty is considerably higher than the numbers suggest. The ridgeline around Gakhosan and Seokgibong Peaks has rocky sections mixed in, so watch your footing in rain or during autumn when fallen leaves pile up — it gets slippery. The trail’s spatial data shows zero separately tallied facilities for hazard zones, viewpoints, or trailheads/exit points, so rather than relying on signage or trail markers, you’re better off using a map app alongside a GPX track.

Distance and Time Estimates

The trails throughout the district add up to 41.09 km, and based on that, the total climbing time comes to 855 minutes, with 599 minutes for the descent. Converted into pace, that works out to roughly 21 minutes per kilometer on the way up — but keep in mind that’s the cumulative figure for walking every single trail in the district, not the time for any one course. Don’t mix the two up. The actual one-way estimate for an individual course runs about 3 hours 25 minutes to 4 hours 6 minutes, meaning a round trip could easily take 7-8 hours or more — this is long-distance hiking territory. If you’re doing one of the shorter courses as a there-and-back trip, a day hike is doable, but if you’re planning a full traverse linking Gakhosan, Minjujisan, Seokgibong, and Samdobong, it’s worth planning an overnight stay somewhere near Mulhan Valley.

Getting There (Public Transit & Parking)

The Minjujisan area is mountainous terrain without much in the way of public transit, so getting there by car is generally the way to go. If you’re relying on public transportation, one option is to take the KTX or a regular train to Yeongdong Station, then transfer to a bus bound for Sangchon-myeon/Mulhan-ri at the Yeongdong Intercity Bus Terminal. Coming from the Muju side, there’s reportedly a route through Muju Intercity Bus Terminal toward Seolcheon-myeon and Domaryeong. That said, buses out here can run infrequently, so definitely check the schedule before you head out.

If you’re driving, the parking area at the Domaryeong trailhead or the lots near Mulhan Valley seem to be the go-to spots. That said, the trail’s spatial data shows zero recorded parking lots or bus stops for this area, so the on-the-ground reality may not quite match what’s on paper — worth double-checking current routes and parking availability on KakaoMap or Naver Map before you go.

Season, Weather & Gear

Spring and fall are the best times to hike here, and fall in particular is hard to beat — the foliage plus relatively stable weather make for great ridge views. In summer, the shaded valley sections take some of the edge off the heat, but most of the routes run long and there are no drinking fountains marked on the trail spatial data, so bring plenty of water. During monsoon season, the stream can rise quickly, so be careful on the walking paths near Mulhan Valley. In winter, snow and ice build up easily on the summit ridge, so crampons, gaiters, and cold-weather gear are essential — and since daylight is shorter, plan for an early start and give yourself plenty of buffer time to get back down. Across all seasons, the basics still apply: hiking boots, plenty of water and emergency food, a map app, a headlamp, and rain gear.

Tips for Foreign Travelers

Mt. Minjujisan is romanized as “Mt. Minjujisan” in English, and it’s worth remembering the names of the main peaks — Seokgibong, Samdobong, and Gakhosan — since they’ll help you read the trail signs. As far as I know, this isn’t a national or provincial park requiring a separate entry permit or admission fee, but if you use the facilities at the recreational forest near Mulhan Valley, there may be a separate charge, so it’s best to check locally. Restrooms are marked at several points along both the Domaryeong and Seokgibong trail courses, but there are no drinking water stations (yaksuteo) shown on the trail’s geographic data, so make sure to bring enough water before you set out. Google Maps is often inaccurate for mountain trail information in Korea, so I’d recommend using KakaoMap or Naver Map instead to check the actual route as well as public transit and parking info. Since the course is long with stretches that see very few other hikers, it’s safer to go with companions rather than solo, and to share your hiking plan with someone in advance.
