A Gentle Dirt-Mountain Ridge Around Goshi Cave: Taehwasan in Yeongwol

Overview

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization Photo Gallery (Gongju, Chungcheongnam-do) · KOGL

Taehwasan sits near the Yeongwol area of Gangwon Province, with a summit elevation of about 1,060m according to actual GPX measurements. The official trail data classifies the toughest route in the area as “easy,” suggesting the mountain’s overall terrain is fairly gentle — but don’t let that fool you. The actual hiking route to the summit runs 9–10km one way with a cumulative elevation gain of 760–860m, so it’s a long haul, and the effort it takes is anything but light. It’s a manageable day hike, but I’d recommend heading out early in the morning.

Mountain Shape and Terrain

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization Photo Gallery (Beomseo-eup, Ulju-gun, Ulsan) · Public Nuri

The summit area actually goes by a few different names on the map — Taehwasan Peak (1,051m), Taehwasan Peak (1,041m), Taehwasan Peak (1,023m) — which suggests the ridge up top isn’t one single bump but a string of gently connected high points (the numbers may vary a bit depending on when and where they were surveyed). Overall, this is known more as an earthy “yuksan”-type mountain, dominated by dirt trails and ridgelines, rather than a rocky peak bristling with exposed granite. Trails open onto the mountain from several directions — Heungwol-ri, Palgoe-ri, Jeongyang-ri, and more — so the slopes tend to be fairly gentle all around. Partway along the route you’ll find Taehwasanseong, the remains of what’s said to be an old fortress, adding a bit of history to the hike. And nearby sits Gossi Cave, well known as a natural limestone cave, making this an area where you can combine a hike with some cave sightseeing.

Feel of the Trail

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization Photo Gallery (Beomseo-eup, Ulju-gun, Ulsan) · Public Nuri

The trail typically starts out gentle, winding through forest before the climb gradually steepens. This isn’t a mountain known for dramatic rock ridges, so it’s less about thrilling scrambles and more a test of steady, sustained hiking. Once you’re up on the ridgeline, you get views of the range’s rolling contours alongside the deep forest filling the valleys below — and since the route to the summit runs quite long, pacing yourself really matters here. Come spring, fresh green spreads along the gentle ridges, and in fall it’s the turn of the autumn colors; summer brings deep shade from the canopy that takes some of the heat off, though the humidity can make it feel tougher going than the temperature alone would suggest.

Course 1 — Shelter → Summit

📏 9.25km ⛰️ 857m gain ⏱️ 3:44 ➡️ One-way Difficulty ★★★★★ ⤓ GPX

Route: Shelter → No-Entry Zone → Junction 3 → Junction 7

Getting to the trailhead: This area isn’t served by subway, so you’ll mainly be transferring between intercity and city buses, and parking near the trailhead seems to be limited. If you’re taking public transit, I’d definitely recommend checking bus stop locations and schedules on KakaoMap or Naver Map beforehand.

Highlights & who it’s for: Of the three courses, this trailhead is the least well-known, which makes it a good pick if you’d rather ease into a quiet hike through gentle forest paths from the start. There’s a stretch marked “No Entry,” so it’s safer to check ahead whether that section is actually closed before you head in. The one-way distance and elevation gain are the longest of the three courses, so this one’s best suited to hikers who are confident in their stamina.

Course 2 — Parking Lot → Observation Deck

📏 8.76km ⛰️ 764m gain ⏱️ 3:28 ➡️ One-way Difficulty ★★★★★ ⤓ GPX

Route: Parking lot → Restroom → Trail information board → Observation deck → Junction 9 → Photo spot → Gossi Cave

Trailhead access: This course is named after the parking lot where it starts, but public data doesn’t confirm an official parking facility here, so it’s a good idea to double-check actual parking availability on a map app before you head out. For nearby public transit, plan on transferring buses as your default option.

Highlights & who it’s for: With a restroom and trail information board right at the entrance, this course is relatively easy for first-timers to get oriented on, and the observation deck and photo spot along the way offer some nice views — which makes it the most popular of the three courses, in my view. Since Gossi Cave is near the descent, it’s especially worth considering if you’re planning to combine hiking with cave sightseeing. The overall elevation gain is also the gentlest of the three courses, so it’s a relatively easier outing.

Course 3 — Taehwasan Children’s Forest Experience Center → Taehwasan Summit

📏 10.01km ⛰️ 811m gain ⏱️ 3:51 ➡️ One-way Difficulty ★★★★★ ⤓ GPX

Route: Taehwasan Children’s Forest Experience Center → Junction 6 → Taehwasan Fortress → rest area → Junction 10 → Taehwasan Summit → restroom → Keungol Bus Stop → parking lot

Highlights & who it’s for: With the Children’s Forest Experience Center right at the trailhead, this entrance is known for being especially easy for families to get to. Partway up, you’ll pass the remains of Taehwasan Fortress, adding a bit of history to the hike. The descent is well laid out too, running past the summit down through a restroom, the Keungol Bus Stop, and finally the parking lot — which makes it a convenient course whether you’re planning a there-and-back hike or a full traverse. That said, this is the longest course on the mountain, clocking in at over 10km one way, so give yourself plenty of time.

Getting to the trailhead: The Keungol Bus Stop sits near the descent point, so connecting with public transit is relatively easy — though as with most rural routes, buses don’t run all that frequently, so it’s worth checking the schedule ahead of time.

Difficulty, Terrain & Cautions

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization photo gallery (Taehwa-dong, Jung-gu, Ulsan) · Public Nuri

According to the district’s trail spatial data, the Taehwasan section is officially rated “Easy” at its highest difficulty level, and no separate data is available on the surface material. That said, the actual summit route runs 9–10km one way with a cumulative elevation gain of 760–860m, making it a long-distance, endurance-heavy hike — so I wouldn’t recommend taking the “Easy” label at face value and going in unprepared. On the facilities side, the data lists zero hazard zones and zero designated viewpoints (though Course 2’s GPX track does separately mark a “lookout” and “photo spot”), along with 4 registered trailheads/exit points. Do watch your footing on the dirt sections when it’s rainy, and be extra careful of icy patches in shaded areas during winter.

Distance and Time

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization Photo Gallery (Taehwa-dong, Jung-gu, Ulsan) · KOGL Type 1

The total length of trails within the district (combining the Heungwol-ri, Palgwe-ri, Jeongyang-ri, and other sections) comes to 14.37 km. Keep in mind this isn’t the distance of any single route — it’s the sum of every registered section in the area. Likewise, the 455-minute ascent and 318-minute descent figures in the data are cumulative totals across all those sections combined, not the time it takes to climb and come down one particular trail. Working out an average pace from that cumulative figure gives roughly 32 minutes per km on the way up — about what you’d expect from the leisurely walking speed typical of a gentle, soil-covered mountain. The actual time for the summit-area routes, as shown in the earlier table, runs from about 3 hours 28 minutes to 3 hours 51 minutes one way, meaning a round trip could take somewhere around 7 to 8 hours total. That’s a perfectly manageable day hike, but I’d still recommend setting out early.

Getting There (Public Transit & Parking)

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization Photo Gallery (Taehwa-dong, Jung-gu, Ulsan) · KOGL

The subway doesn’t reach this area, so the usual approach is to take an intercity or city bus to a nearby stop and then walk the rest of the way to the trailhead. There’s a “Neuti” bus stop near the trailhead that you can use as your reference point for planning the walk-in. That said, the public data doesn’t show a dedicated parking lot in the area, so if you’re driving, it’s a good idea to pair it with public transit or check ahead of time whether parking is actually available. For accurate real-time routes and bus schedules, I’d recommend double-checking with KakaoMap or Naver Map before you head out.

Season, Weather & Gear

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization Photo Gallery (Taehwa-dong, Jung-gu, Ulsan) · Public Nuri

Spring (April–May) and fall (September–October) are generally considered the best times to hike this trail. In summer, the dense forest canopy offers some relief from the heat, but humidity runs high, and since there’s no confirmed water fountain along the route, managing your drinking water becomes especially important. In winter, be prepared for icy patches in shaded sections — crampons and gaiters are worth packing. There’s also no confirmed shelter along the way, so if the weather turns bad, deciding to head back down early is key. For essential gear, bring ankle-supporting hiking boots, plenty of water (this is a long out-and-back course, so 1.5–2L or more is recommended), a waterproof jacket, and a headlamp in case you start early or come down late.

Tips for Foreign Travelers

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization Photo Gallery (Taehwa-dong, Jung-gu, Ulsan) · KOGL Type 1

The summit can be romanized as “Taehwasan Summit,” and one of the trailheads, the toddler forest experience center, as “Taehwasan Forest Experience Center for Children.” This is an ordinary mountain with no confirmed special permit or entrance fee requirements, but it’s still a good idea to check the local forest authority’s notices before you go, just in case of any access restrictions. Restrooms can be found near the trailheads and summit area on Course 2 (parking lot → observation deck) and Course 3 (toddler forest experience center → Taehwasan summit), but there’s no dedicated drinking water station anywhere on the mountain, so be sure to bring enough water with you before setting out. For navigation, we’d strongly recommend using a Korean map app like KakaoMap or Naver Map rather than Google Maps — they’re far more accurate when it comes to trail routes and public transit information. Given how long the one-way course is, check your phone battery and signal strength beforehand, and leave yourself plenty of time to come back down so you’re not caught hiking after dark.
