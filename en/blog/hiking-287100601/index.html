Overview

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization Tourist Photo Gallery (Hwasun-gun, Jeollanam-do) · KOGL Type 1

Manisan (摩尼山) sits in Hwado-myeon, Ganghwa-gun, Incheon, and it’s best known as Ganghwado’s signature historic hike thanks to Chamseongdan, the altar at the summit said to have been built for Dangun’s heaven-worship rites marking the founding of the nation. Based on measured trail data, the peak tops out at 491m — not a big mountain by any means, but the mix of ridgeline and rocky sections means the ups and downs feel more substantial than the modest elevation suggests. Trail difficulty tops out at “moderate” in the data, and with one-way distances of roughly 3–7km depending on the route, it’s an easy day hike you can knock out and be back well before dark.

Terrain and Landscape

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization Photo Gallery (Korean Folk Village, Yongin, Gyeonggi-do) · KOGL

Manisan sits in a spot with sweeping views of the Yellow Sea and the mudflats along Ganghwa’s southern tip, and its terrain is a mix of craggy rock ridges and softer, forested slopes. The summit itself isn’t a single sharp peak — it’s registered across several separate points. Survey data actually lists three distinct spots: Manisan Summit (491m), Manisan Summit (470m), and Manisan Summit (488m). The way to make sense of that is to picture the area around Chamseongdan altar as a gently connected cluster of rocky knobs and the old altar site itself, so depending on exactly where the measurement was taken, the elevation reading shifts slightly. On the way up to the top, granite bedrock breaks through the surface in several places, and along the fork routes in particular you’ll find markers reading “slippery,” “caution: fall risk,” and “danger zone — fall accidents reported” — a clear sign that some sections involve steep, exposed rock. By contrast, the lower ground toward Hamheodongcheon valley is heavily forested and much gentler underfoot, with a campground tucked in alongside the stream.

Feel of the Trail

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization photo gallery (Korean Folk Village, Yongin, Gyeonggi-do) · KOGL

One thing that stands out about hiking Manisan is how sharply the mood shifts between the first half and the second. Head in toward Hamheodongcheon and you’re under a shaded canopy that hugs the valley for a good while — pleasantly cool even in summer. But as you climb closer to the summit, the trees thin out, granite starts poking through, and the view suddenly opens up. Take the Manisan-ipgu course and you’re rewarded, near Chamseongdan and the peak, with a wide-open view down over the Yellow Sea and its mudflats — it’s a short hike, but one that still delivers that summit-day payoff of a real sense of achievement paired with a proper view. Spring and fall bring especially clear ridge views, summer thickens the valley into deep green, and winter blankets the rocky sections in snow and ice — so even though it’s the same course, how hard it feels can swing wildly depending on the season.

Course 1 — Entrance Restroom → Summit

📏 6.07km ⛰️ 463m gain ⏱️ 2:17 ➡️ One-way Difficulty ★★★☆☆ ⤓ GPX

Route: Hamheodongcheon → Hamheodongcheon parking lot → Hamheodongcheon Parking Lots 2 & 3 → Campground Meeting Point Supermarket → Entrance restroom → Parking Lot 1 → Ere Garden → Cloud Valley Supermarket → Rest area → Entrance ticket booth → Snack shop → Restroom → Campground 2 → Fork in the trail → Hamheojeong Pavilion

Getting to the trailhead: The Hamheodongcheon trailhead is reached from the Hwado-myeon side of Ganghwa County. There’s a Hamheodongcheon bus stop nearby, so you can get there by local bus from downtown Ganghwa (Ganghwa Terminal). Exact schedules and the stop’s precise location are worth double-checking locally before you go.

What it’s like / who it’s for: The early stretch is packed with amenities — a valley, a campground, a snack shop, a supermarket — so it’s easy to rest up or restock before and after the hike. The trail starts along a shaded valley path and gains elevation gradually, making it a good pick if you’d rather ease into a hike than get thrown straight into a steep climb. After the fork, the second half shifts into more of a ridge feel on the way up to Hamheojeong Pavilion.

Course 2 — Manisan Trailhead → Summit

📏 6.84km ⛰️ 445m gain ⏱️ 2:27 🔁 Loop Difficulty ★★★☆☆ ⤓ GPX

Waypoints: Manisan trailhead → Dangol Restaurant → Manisan tourist area free parking lot → restroom → GS25 → Manisan National Tourist Site entrance → Manisan trailhead ticket booth → fork → Manisan spring water spot → rest area → Chamseongdan Altar → summit → Manisan summit → viewpoint → viewpoint (mudflat view)

Getting to the trailhead: You can reach the start via the Manisan-ipgu stop or the Hwado Terminal stop — typically by transferring to a local county bus at Ganghwa Terminal and riding it to the entrance of Manisan National Tourist Site. There’s a free parking lot at the Manisan tourist area along the route, but it can get crowded on weekends during peak season, so it’s worth considering public transit as well.

Highlights and who it’s for: With the entrance to Manisan National Tourist Site, a ticket booth, restaurants, and a convenience store all in place, this is the most accessible and convenient of the courses — the flagship route, really. You’ll pass Chamseongdan Altar just before the summit, and from both the summit and a separate viewpoint you get views out toward the Yellow Sea and the mudflats. It’s the course I’d recommend most for first-time visitors to Manisan or for anyone combining the hike with a visit to Chamseongdan. One thing to note: there’s a spot along the way labeled “Manisan spring water,” but whether it’s actually drinkable and properly maintained is something you’d need to check on site — better to bring your own water just in case.

Course 3 — Fork → Summit

📏 3.23km ⛰️ 446m gain ⏱️ 1:33 ➡️ One-way Difficulty ★★★☆☆ ⤓ GPX

Route: Fork → restrooms → Heart-Shaped Rock (“Love Heart Rock”) → slippery-slope caution → fall-risk caution → fall-accident danger zone → fall-accident risk → Hamheodongcheon Valley → campground rest area → campground rest area

Trailhead access: This section starts at the fork connecting to Courses 1 and 2, so people rarely approach it via its own separate transit route — instead, most walk in from Hamheodongcheon or the Manisan entrance and pick up this stretch along the way.

Highlights & who it’s for: Of the three courses, this one’s the shortest in distance, but its defining feature is the run of warning signs — “slippery slope,” “fall-risk caution,” “fall-accident danger zone” — one after another. With so much elevation change packed into so little distance, the grade feels steep, and the rocky ridge sections demand real caution. It suits hikers with some experience better than beginners, and if you’re just starting out, it’s best to skip this stretch in rainy weather or during winter.

Difficulty, Terrain & Cautions

The distances in the table above are the combined total (28.27km) of several trail segments registered within Ganghwa County, grouped by administrative district — not the length of any single course. In the actual measured course data, all three summit-area courses (measured from the entrance restrooms, the Manisan entrance, and the fork in the trail) are rated ★★★☆☆, so they feel noticeably tougher than the county-wide average would suggest. Much of the terrain is bare granite bedrock, which offers decent grip even on clear days, but the slip risk climbs sharply right after rain or when it ices over in winter. In particular, the spots marked for slip hazards, fall hazards, and fall-risk zones along the fork-in-the-trail course call for either a detour or extra caution when passing through in wet weather. The data doesn’t separately track hazard zones, scenic viewpoints, or official trailhead/exit POIs, but the waypoint list for the Manisan entrance course does show two viewpoints near the summit — a general scenic viewpoint and one overlooking the tidal flats.

Distance and Time Needed

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization Photo Gallery (Korean Folk Village, Yongin, Gyeonggi-do) · KOGL Type 1

Manisan is a solid day-hike destination, with one-way distances ranging from 3 to 7 km — you can knock it out in half a day or comfortably within a single day round-trip. On the main route, from the Manisan entrance to the summit, it’s about 2 hours 27 minutes one way (6.84 km). From the entrance restroom to the summit, figure roughly 2 hours 17 minutes (6.07 km). And from the fork in the trail to the summit, it’s a shorter 1 hour 33 minutes (3.23 km). Round trip, most people are looking at somewhere between 3 and 5 hours total. If you wanted to cover every trail in the district (28.27 km combined across all segments), the data shows a cumulative 558 minutes going up and 386 minutes coming down — but that’s the sum across the entire trail network, not the time for any single course, so don’t mistake it for how long one particular route takes. Converting that into an average pace works out to about 20 minutes per kilometer on the ascent, which is a handy benchmark: you can apply it to the one-way times above and get a rough sense of how the hike will feel given your own fitness level.

Getting There (Public Transit & Parking)

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization Photo Gallery (Korean Folk Village, Yongin, Gyeonggi-do) · KOGL Type 1

If you’re taking public transit, catch a local bus from Ganghwa Terminal heading toward Manisan, then get off at stops like Hamheodongcheon, Daesa Village, Manisan Trailhead, or Hwado Terminal to reach the trail entrance. Coming from Seoul, the usual route is to take a bus to Ganghwa-do from Sinchon or Incheon, arrive at Ganghwa Terminal, and transfer from there. Buses run pretty infrequently, so it’s worth checking real-time routes and schedules on KakaoMap or Naver Map beforehand. If you’re driving, there’s a free parking lot at the Manisan tourist site that shows up along the Manisan Trailhead route — but since it’s not an officially registered parking POI in the public data, I’d play it safe and check the lot’s size and how crowded it gets during peak season once you’re actually there. If you’re heading in via Hamheodongcheon, you’ll also spot the Hamheodongcheon parking lot and Lots 2 and 3 along the way, which can serve as a backup.

Season, Weather & Gear

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization Photo Gallery (6 Haean-namro 1114beon-gil, Hwado-myeon, Ganghwa-gun, Incheon) · Gonggongnuri (Korea Open Government License)

Spring (April–May) and fall (September–November) are generally considered the best seasons to hike Manisan — the views along the ridge and summit are clear and the temperatures stay mild. In summer, the shaded valley sections offer some relief from the heat, but you’ll want to be ready for humidity and bugs, and watch your footing on the rocky ridges since sweat can make the rock slick. In winter, ice tends to form on the summit’s rocky sections and around Chamseongdan, so crampons and cold-weather gear are essential — and on the fork-trail route in particular, the fall-risk section is one you should think twice about crossing in winter. As for basic gear, bring hiking boots with good ankle support, a change of clothes, plenty of water, and a wind/cold-weather jacket suited to the season — and in winter, add crampons and gaiters, while in summer, insect repellent is worth packing too.

Tips for Foreign Travelers

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization Photo Gallery (Onsu-ri, Gilsang-myeon, Ganghwa-gun, Incheon) · Public Nuri

The romanized name for Manisan is “Manisan,” and the ancient altar at the summit, used for heaven-worship rituals, is labeled “Chamseongdan.” The main trailheads are marked as things like “Manisan-ipgu” and “Hamheodongcheon.” Since this is a provincial/county-managed area rather than a national park, you don’t need a separate hiking permit, but there may be an admission fee to enter the tourist area, so it’s worth checking at the local ticket booths (Manisan-ipgu ticket booth, entrance ticket booth). Restrooms are available at the trailheads for each course (the entrance restroom near the Manisan National Tourist Site, and one near the trail junction), but there’s no confirmed drinking water source above the midpoint of the mountain, so make sure to bring plenty of water before you set out. Google Maps can be unreliable here, so I’d recommend using KakaoMap or Naver Map instead — they’re also the safer bet for checking bus times and routes. For safety, pay close attention to the warning signs at the junction course (watch for slippery spots, falls, etc.), and given how early it gets dark, it’s best to avoid starting your hike too late in the afternoon.
