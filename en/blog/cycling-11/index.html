The East Coast (Gangwon) Bike Path: Route, Distance, and Certification Centers

Overview

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization photo gallery (Buk-myeon, Inje-gun, Gangwon State) · KOGL

The Donghaean Gangwon Bike Path (East Coast Gangwon Bike Path) starts just south of the Civilian Control Line at the northernmost tip of the Korean peninsula and runs south along Gangwon’s eastern sea. If the inland routes like the Han River or Nakdong River trails have that monotonous, flat rhythm of following a river and its levees, this one is a completely different animal. The sea stays glued to your right the whole way, and you’re constantly crossing marine terraces and harbors, which means short, punchy climbs and descents over and over. To your left you’ve got the ridgelines of Seorak and Taebaek; to your right, the horizon opens up. That’s why it gets called the most scenically dense of all the Korean cross-country routes.

There are basically four ways to make use of it.

One: a three-day or four-day through-ride. This is the standard way to enjoy the route. Start in Goseong and break it into daily legs — Sokcho/Yangyang, then Gangneung, then Donghae/Samcheok — and you’re looking at 70–90 km a day, which leaves room for sightseeing and proper meals. Lodging is densely packed along the coastline, so booking is relatively painless.

Two: single-day section rides. You don’t have to ride the whole thing to get a lot out of it. Pick cities the trains and buses reach as your start and end points — Sokcho to Gangneung, or Gangneung to Samcheok — and you’ve got a 60–80 km day trip. The sections around Gangneung in particular have good road surfaces and easy access from town, which makes them a good fit for beginners and family rides.

Three: a folding bike (Brompton or minivelo) plus public transit. Fold it into a bag and trains and express buses get a whole lot easier. Take the train to Gangneung Station or Donghae Station, ride only the section you want, then hop back on the train — for travelers short on time, that’s the most efficient approach going.

Four: collecting cross-country certification stamps. This route is part of the national cross-country certification program. Collect stamps from all 12 certification centers and you’ve officially completed the Donghaean (Gangwon) section, and plenty of riders combine it with the other through-routes with an eye on the full Grand Slam.

Course Details

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization photo gallery (Inje-gun, Gangwon State) · KOGL

The starting point is the Unification Observatory certification center in Goseong. There’s real symbolism in setting off from the northernmost spot a civilian can reach by bike, right up against the line dividing North and South. From there you head south past Hwajinpo and Geojin, then onto the Bukcheon Railway Bridge section — an old rail line converted into a bike path. Next comes Bongpo and downtown Sokcho, past Yeonggeumjeong (the pavilion perched on the rocks) and around Cheongchoho Lake and Sokcho Port.

The Yangyang stretch delivers one coastal view after another around Naksan and Hajodae. Here the route alternates between coastal road and dedicated bike path, with the sea appearing again and again through gaps in the pine forest. Once you cross into Gangneung, you pass the fish-market bustle of Jumunjin Port, the wide promenades of Gyeongpo Lake and Gyeongpo Beach, and the Anmok coffee street. South of Gangneung, the marine terraces at Jeongdongjin and the path running alongside the railway are the standouts.

In Donghae you ride through Mangsang and Mukho, and crossing into Samcheok you hit the strange rock formations around Chuam Candlestick Rock and the cliffside stretch along Isabu-gil. The route ends at the southern edge of Samcheok, at the point on the coast where Gangwon meets North Gyeongsang Province.

Road surface varies a lot section to section. Near the cities and through the beach parks you get well-paved dedicated bike paths; the stretches linking ports and villages are coastal roads shared with cars, mixed in with shared paths where you’re alongside pedestrians. Rather than expecting the “dedicated path the whole way” you’d get on the inland river routes, it’s more accurate to think of this as a route that alternates between bike-only and regular roads. And there are definitely hills. Nothing you’d call a mountain pass, but every port you pass tends to come with a short climb attached, so total elevation gain adds up to far more than on the flat river routes.

Local Eats

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization photo gallery (Inje-gun, Gangwon State) · KOGL

The Gangwon east coast is basically one long food belt from end to end. Rather than pinning your hopes on a specific restaurant, plan around what each area is known for — you’ll rarely be disappointed.

  • Around Goseong: pollock dishes, sandfish (dorumuk) stew, and grilled-fish set meals are the usual suspects. Shops are sparse right after the start, so it’s worth sorting out breakfast before you set off.
  • Sokcho: squid sundae, dakgangjeong (sweet crispy fried chicken), mulhoe (cold raw fish soup), and the Hamgyeong-style cooking around Abai Village are the headliners. It’s also one of the best cities on the whole route for restocking and finding somewhere to eat.
  • Yangyang: seopguk (mussel soup) and mulhoe, plus pine mushroom dishes in autumn, tend to get billed as the local specialties.
  • Gangneung: Chodang sundubu (soft tofu), potato ongsimi (potato dumpling soup), jang kalguksu (noodle soup in soybean paste broth), and of course the coffee culture. This is usually the most popular mid-ride rest stop.
  • Donghae and Samcheok: winter fish dishes get top billing — gomchiguk (snailfish soup), snow crab and red crab, and mulgomtang.

A warning about resupply gaps: the far northern stretch of Goseong, some coastal sections between Yangyang and Gangneung, and the cliffside stretch at the southern end of Samcheok all have wide gaps between convenience stores and restaurants. Beachfront shops often close in winter and the off-season, so get into the habit of topping up water and snacks whenever you pass through a town.

Supplies & Facilities

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization photo gallery (Inje-gun, Gangwon State) · KOGL

Across the full route you’ll find 12 certification centers, 118 restrooms, 22 water stations, and 3 air pumps.

118 restrooms is genuinely generous by cross-country route standards. That’s thanks to the local geography — every beach and harbor has a public restroom. The 22 water stations leave you some breathing room too, but they’re clustered around beachfront parks and urban stretches, so I’d recommend carrying two bottles or more. With only 3 air pumps out there, a portable pump and a puncture repair kit are non-negotiable. On this route, you need to be able to fix things yourself.

The certification centers are what give the route its backbone. Starting from the Unification Observatory, they’re placed at the area’s signature spots — Bukcheon Railway Bridge, Yeonggeumjeong, Hajodae, Jeongdongjin, Chuam Candlestick Rock — so collecting stamps doubles as a sightseeing itinerary. The exact list and order of all 12 is printed inside the certification passport, so it’s worth checking the moment you get yours and spreading them across your daily plan.

How the certification system works: at each center, you stamp the matching box in your passport. Staffed centers keep operating hours, but most unstaffed ones are accessible 24 hours. Stamps do smudge and ink does dry out, so it’s safer to snap a photo right after stamping as a backup record.

The Ride Home

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization photo gallery (Inje-gun, Gangwon State) · KOGL

This is a one-way route, so figuring out how you’ll get back is honestly half the trip. Riding the whole thing as a round trip means 484km, which doubles your schedule — so most people take public transport home.

By train (my top pick): Gangneung Station connects directly to Seoul by KTX, and the Donghae Station and Samcheok Beach Station area is served by Donghae Line trains. Stations close to the route, like Jeongdongjin and Mukho Port, make handy bail-out points mid-ride. Here’s the general picture on bringing a bike aboard.

  • Mugunghwa, ITX, and other conventional trains: These will often let you bring a bike on, and some trains have bike racks in certain cars. It varies by train set and line, though, so check ahead.
  • KTX: Assume you can’t roll a fully assembled bike on. The rule is a folding bike, folded and packed in a proper bag (wheel covers or a full cover) so it fits luggage dimensions. A road bike bag with the wheels off gets treated the same way.
  • Either way, rush hour and weekends get crowded, so give yourself plenty of time boarding and getting off, and use the space behind the seats or at the end of the car.

By bus: Express and intercity bus terminals in Gangneung, Donghae, Samcheok, and Sokcho run frequent services back to the Seoul metro area. The usual approach is to fold or break down the bike, bag it, and stow it in the luggage bay underneath — though whether it actually gets on can come down to the driver’s judgment. The tidier your packing job, the less likely you are to get turned away. Don’t plan on walking up with a fully assembled bike.

City and rural buses: Local bus stops linking the coastal villages with stations and terminals are reasonably dense along the route. Loading a bike is generally a no-go, though, so think of these as a last resort for when your legs are完全 shot.

Riding back: Doing the full 484km round trip on the bike means budgeting another 4–6 days minimum. Heading north, the seasonal winds shift and the ride can feel like a different beast — so if you’re set on the round trip, leave yourself serious slack in both your schedule and your legs.

Safety and Cautions

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization photo gallery (Inje-gun, Gangwon State) · KOGL

  • Shared paths and pedestrians: On stretches where the route doubles as a seaside promenade, slow right down. In peak summer, the beaches, Gyeongpo and Anmok in Gangneung, and downtown Sokcho get extremely crowded with people on foot. Ring your bell or call out ahead of time, and keep your speed low.
  • Sections mixed with regular roads: The coastal roads linking the ports carry trucks and tour buses. Stay in the right-hand lane and keep a rear light on at all times so drivers behind can see you.
  • Tunnels and shoulders: Some sections take you through tunnels and along narrow shoulders. Switch on your front and rear lights before you enter, and wearing something reflective makes a big difference.
  • Ice: In winter, the coast ices up easily on shaded cliffside stretches and on bridges. If the road merely looks wet, treat it as black ice.
  • Water and dehydration: The sea breeze dries your sweat fast, which makes dehydration hard to notice. Drink on a schedule rather than waiting until you’re thirsty, and add electrolytes in summer.
  • Night riding: Much of the coastal route has no street lighting. Avoid riding after sunset if you can, and if you can’t, a bright front light isn’t optional.

Season, Weather, and Gear

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization photo gallery (Inje-gun, Gangwon State) · KOGL

The seasons I’d recommend are May–June and September–October. The temperatures are mild, the sea is at its most beautiful, and most of the shops along the beaches are open for business.

  • Spring: Good riding conditions, but plenty of windy days, and early spring brings big swings between daytime and nighttime temps. A thin windbreaker earns its keep.
  • Summer: Heat, UV, and peak-season crowds all crank up the difficulty. Safer to set off early in the morning and take a long break around midday. During monsoon and typhoon season, watch out especially for strong coastal winds.
  • Fall: The most reliable season. Just remember the sun sets early, so be conservative about how far you plan to ride in a day.
  • Winter: North winds, snow, and ice stack up and the difficulty climbs sharply. I wouldn’t recommend it without proper cold-weather gear.

Essential gear: helmet, front and rear lights, portable pump, spare tube and puncture repair kit, multi-tool, two water bottles, snacks, sunscreen, windbreaker, a bit of cash (for the smaller shops), and a power bank. And let me stress it again — there are only three air pumps on the entire route. Repair tools aren’t optional here. They’re mandatory.

Tips for International Travelers

Photo: Korea Tourism Organization photo gallery (Inje-gun, Gangwon State) · KOGL

① Romanized route names Donghaean (Gangwon) Bike Path / East Coast Gangwon Bicycle Road. Key place names: Goseong, Sokcho, Yangyang, Gangneung, Donghae, Samcheok, Jeongdongjin, and the Unification Observatory.

② A practical guide to the Certification System This route is part of Korea’s cross-country cycling certification program.

  • Bike Passport: Buying one in person at a staffed certification center is by far the most realistic option. The official website is Korean-only, and pre-ordering from abroad is tough because of foreign card payments and Korea’s identity verification (ID) requirements. Just use a staffed center in a larger city like Gangneung, or one on any of the main cross-country routes.
  • Unstaffed certification centers: These are small booths shaped like red phone boxes. Open the door and you’ll find a rubber stamp hanging inside on a string. Press it onto the right center’s box on your passport’s page for that route, and you’re done. It’s free, there’s nobody on duty, and most are accessible 24 hours.
  • Completing a section: Collect every stamp on a section and you get a certification sticker marking it complete; finish multiple routes and you can receive a completion certificate and a medal. This is the standard procedure laid out by the cross-country certification program.
  • Tip: Stamp locations are easy to miss, so mark the centers you’ll pass that day on your map each morning — it saves you from having to double back.

③ If you’re arriving without a bike In Seoul, the public bike-share Ttareungi has an English app and accepts foreign cards, but it only operates within Seoul city limits, so it’s no use on this route. For the Gangwon east coast section, the usual options are private bike shops in Gangneung and Sokcho, guesthouse rental services, and rental outfits in the greater Seoul area that carry road bikes and MTBs built for distance. If you’re planning the full route, a long-term rental from the Seoul area brought over by train is the safer bet. Bikes from beachside rental shops are generally meant for short rides and aren’t up to 242 km.

④ Map apps Google Maps’ cycling directions aren’t very accurate in Korea and often fail to reflect the certified routes. Use KakaoMap or Naver Map in bicycle mode instead. Both offer English interfaces and are far more accurate for bike-only paths and for finding certification centers. There are cliff and tunnel stretches where you’ll lose signal, so save offline maps or a GPX file ahead of time — and note that searching with Korean place names tends to be more accurate than Romanized ones.
